EMOTION GUIDE: FEAR

PURPOSE
Use this guide when the detected emotional state is fear.
The aim is to reduce threat arousal, support grounding in the present moment, and encourage one safe and manageable coping action.

EVIDENCE BASIS
This guide is grounded in:
- WHO: Doing What Matters in Times of Stress (2020)
- NHS: Get help with anxiety, fear or panic
- NHS: Breathing exercises for stress
- NHS inform: Grounding exercises
- CDC: Managing Difficult Emotions
- CDC: Improve Your Emotional Well-Being
- APA: Anxiety topic guidance
- Gross (1998): emotion regulation framework
- CBT background literature on identifying and challenging fears

CORE INTERPRETATION
When the detected emotion is fear, the response should assume that the user may be experiencing threat sensitivity, physical tension, worry, uncertainty, or a sense of being unsafe.
The first priority is to help the user feel more grounded and steady before suggesting reflection, problem solving, or action.
The response should not diagnose anxiety or panic, and it should not amplify the perceived threat.

RESPONSE GOALS
- Help the user orient to the present moment.
- Reduce physical arousal through slow breathing, grounding, or relaxation.
- Support a sense of safety using simple and immediate actions.
- Encourage practical next steps only after the user has calmed.
- Keep the message short and non-clinical.

RECOMMENDED SUPPORT DIRECTIONS
A good direction for fear is to help the user notice the present environment instead of staying focused only on the feared thought.
A good direction for fear is to suggest slow, gentle breathing so the body can begin to settle.
A good direction for fear is to encourage grounding through the senses when the fear feels overwhelming.
A good direction for fear is to suggest moving to a safer, calmer, or more comfortable place when that is realistic.
A good direction for fear is to encourage one small practical step rather than asking the user to solve the whole situation immediately.
A good direction for fear is to gently reduce catastrophic thinking without arguing with the user or dismissing the feeling.
A good direction for fear is to encourage contacting a trusted person if the user appears overwhelmed or alone.

GOOD ACTION TYPES
A good action for fear is to take a slow breath in and a longer breath out while relaxing the shoulders.
A good action for fear is to name five things the user can see around them to reconnect with the present moment.
A good action for fear is to place both feet on the ground and notice the contact with the floor.
A good action for fear is to step into a calmer or safer space if the current setting feels too intense.
A good action for fear is to loosen tense muscles in the jaw, hands, and shoulders while breathing slowly.
A good action for fear is to write down the specific worry and one small next step when the user is calm enough.
A good action for fear is to reach out to a trusted person when the fear feels difficult to manage alone.

STYLE RULES
- Use steady, reassuring, non-dramatic wording.
- Sound calm and practical rather than overly emotional.
- Validate the feeling without confirming that the feared outcome is true.
- Prefer present-moment language such as "right now" and "one small step".
- Keep the response short and easy to follow.

AVOID
- Do not say "there is nothing to be afraid of," because that can feel dismissive.
- Do not intensify the fear by focusing on danger or worst-case outcomes.
- Do not give complex reasoning while the user is highly fearful.
- Do not encourage avoidance as the main long-term strategy.
- Do not push exposure-like advice in a brief nudge system.
- Do not diagnose anxiety, panic, trauma, phobia, or any clinical condition.
- Do not suggest risky actions or unsafe escape behavior.

CONTEXT ADAPTATION HINTS
When it is night time, support for fear should lean toward calm, low-stimulation grounding, comfort, and preparing for rest.
When it is morning or daytime, support for fear can include grounding first and then one small practical step for the day.
When the user is at home, support for fear can suggest a quiet space, slow breathing, or a comforting routine.
When the user is at work, university, or another study setting, support for fear should suggest discreet grounding such as relaxed breathing, feet on the floor, or a short break.
When the user is outside or commuting, support for fear should prioritize safety, present-moment awareness, and moving to a calmer place if needed.
When the weather is stormy or rainy, support for fear should prefer indoor grounding and reassurance rather than outdoor movement.
When the weather is hot, support for fear can include hydration, cooling down, and slower breathing when appropriate.
When it is a weekday, support for fear should stay brief and practical so it does not interrupt responsibilities.
When it is a weekend, support for fear can allow slightly more time for recovery, connection, journaling, or a calming routine.
